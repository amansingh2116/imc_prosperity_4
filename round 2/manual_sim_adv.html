Why each number is what it is
1
Research ≈ 15% — The research function R(x) = 200k·ln(1+x)/ln(101) grows at 1/(1+x). Its marginal value falls below Scale's flat marginal value at r ≈ 23% (when sp=0). As sp increases, the remaining budget shrinks, so the optimal r* also shrinks — to ~15% when sp=40%. At 15%, you capture 60% of maximum research value. Beyond 20%, every percent is 30× less efficient than the first percent.
2
Scale ≈ 45% — Scale is perfectly linear (no diminishing returns). After fixing r* and sp, every remaining percent goes to Scale. With sp=40% and r=15%, that leaves exactly s=45%. No further optimization possible or needed — just fill Scale with everything left.
3
Speed ≈ 40% — This is the hard number. The realistic player mixture has median competitor speed ≈ 36%. At sp=40% you beat ~58% of the field → E[mult] ≈ 0.58. The PnL curve peaks here because: increasing sp further raises the multiplier (good) but destroys R×S faster than the multiplier gain compensates (bad). The exact crossover point is sp=39–41% for the realistic distribution.
4
100% budget usage — Unspent budget is free Scale. The cost of 1% extra Scale is 500 XIRECs but returns R(15)·S(1)·m ≈ 120k·0.07·0.58 ≈ 4,872. Every unspent percent destroys ~4,372 XIRECs of expected PnL. The screenshot showing 75% usage is costing roughly 50,000 XIRECs in expectation.
What your range of 15–35% gets wrong
The monotonicity trap: Within your stated range (15–35%), the expected PnL is monotonically increasing — E[PnL] at sp=15 is 79k, at sp=35 is 158k. When the function keeps rising at your stated upper bound, the bound is in the wrong place. This is the clearest mathematical proof your range is off.
The self-defeating assumption: You correctly believe competitors invest heavily in speed (~35–45%). But then you cap yourself at 35%, which by your own model gives you a below-median rank and mult ≈ 0.40–0.50. The belief and the action are contradictory. If median is 36%, you need sp > 36% to rank above it.
The true Nash equilibrium (surprising result): Formal game theory says the symmetric Nash equilibrium is sp* ≈ 93%. This is because the marginal benefit of speed (0.8 × density × RS) exceeds the marginal cost (500/%) at every point up to sp≈93%. But this is the pure-theory answer assuming perfectly rational players — in practice, bounded rationality stops people far short of this. The Level-k analysis (Level 1→36%, Level 2→44%, Level 3→51%) shows the realistic equilibrium is somewhere in the 40–55% range among analytical players.

Game theory — formal and bounded rationality
Formal Nash equilibrium: sp* ≈ 93%
At any symmetric equilibrium sp*, the marginal benefit of +1% speed = 0.8 × f(sp*) × RS(sp*). The cost is always 500/%. For an internal equilibrium to exist: RS(sp*) = 500/(0.8×f). With Normal(σ=12), f=0.033, this requires RS=18,800 — which only occurs at sp≈93%. Conclusion: the pure Nash pushes everyone to near-maximal speed investment, destroying R×S and creating a coordination failure.
Boundary equilibria:
sp=0% is unstable: anyone can defect to sp=1% and get mult jump from 0.1→~0.9 for nearly free. sp=100% gives R×S=0, PnL= −50k always. The game has no interior Nash except near the boundary at 93%, making it inherently unstable and sensitive to bounded rationality.

Each level escalates. The competition is unstable — every smart player wants to be slightly above the crowd. This is the "arms race" structure. The question is: which level is the actual player field?
Practical equilibrium (bounded rationality):
IMC Prosperity players are L1–L2 thinkers on average. Top 16% players are L2–L3. This places the realistic equilibrium around sp=40–50% for the full field, or sp=44–55% for the top tier. Being a L2 player (sp=40–44%) against a mostly L1 field is the sweet spot.

Why the arms race stabilizes before 93%
1
Cognitive limits: Most players don't iterate beyond L1–L2. Once you've done the analysis once ("others will invest X, I should invest X+ε"), you stop. The population stabilizes far from the formal Nash.
2
Risk aversion: Investing 60%+ in speed is psychologically uncomfortable. Players anchor on "don't put too much in one thing." This creates a de-facto coordination on moderate values.
3
Diminishing signal: Beyond sp=50%, each additional percent buys very little extra rank (you're already beating most people). Players who think in terms of "rough tiers" stop well before the formal Nash.
